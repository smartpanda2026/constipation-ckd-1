🌿 Step 1 — Non-Pharmacologic Measures

Always start here. These measures are foundational and associated with lower mortality in CKD, though fluid and potassium restrictions may limit feasibility.

Dietary Fiber

  • Maximize tolerated dietary fiber within potassium and phosphorus restrictions.
  • Goal: 20–38 g/day if feasible.
  • Low-potassium fiber sources: apples, berries, cabbage, cauliflower, green beans, carrots, whole wheat bread, barley.
  • Add fiber gradually to avoid bloating and gas.
  • Ground flaxseed (1–2 tbsp/day) can be added to yogurt or cereals.
  • Caution: Very large amounts of fiber are unsuitable for patients on fluid restriction.

Fluid Optimization

  • Optimize fluid intake within prescribed restriction limits.
  • Patients on fluid restriction may not tolerate bulk-forming agents.

Physical Activity

  • Encourage physical activity — aim for 30 minutes/day as tolerated.
  • Walking, stretching, gardening, or stationary cycling all help promote bowel motility.

Bowel Habits

  • Go to the washroom as soon as the urge is felt.
  • Establish a regular time for bowel movements every 1–2 days.
  • Goal: Bowel movement every 1–2 days.

Medication Review

Review and minimize constipating culprit medications where clinically possible: phosphate binders, oral iron, potassium binders, diuretics, opioids, antihistamines, psychotropics, calcium channel blockers.

💊 Step 2 — First-Line Laxatives

Considered safe in ESRD at unchanged doses — bisacodyl, docusate (sodium), senna, lactulose, and macrogols/PEG.

Bisacodyl Stimulant 5–10 mg PO daily PRN 6–12 h Safe
*PEG caution: Should be used with caution because of the water volume required with intake. The American Gastroenterological Association recommends avoiding PEG without direct physician supervision due to risk of fluid and electrolyte disturbances. Lactulose is the most frequently prescribed agent in advanced CKD/ESRD and is relatively safe; it may also favorably modulate gut microbiota and suppress uremic toxins.
🚫 Do NOT use in renal impairment:
  • Magnesium-containing laxatives (magnesium hydroxide/citrate) — risk of hypermagnesemia.
  • Phosphate-containing laxatives (sodium phosphate) — risk of acute phosphate nephropathy and hyperphosphatemia.
  • Bulk-forming agents (psyllium, methylcellulose, ispaghula husk) — often poorly tolerated due to fluid restriction; polycarbophil adds calcium burden.

Maintenance Strategy

  • For chronic constipation, consider scheduled maintenance therapy with regular lactulose or PEG 3350 (± docusate only if hard stool).
  • Titrate the scheduled laxative regimen to achieve a regular BM pattern of q1–2 days.
  • If no BM after 3 days on first-line therapy → proceed to Step 3.

🔬 Step 3 — Second-Line & Refractory Options

For patients not responding to first-line laxatives, or those with concurrent hyperphosphatemia.

Guanylate Cyclase-C Agonists

  • Linaclotide — 72–145 μg PO daily. Very limited systemic absorption; appears safe in CKD.
  • Plecanatide — 3 mg PO daily. Very limited systemic absorption; appears safe in CKD.

Chloride Channel Activator

  • Lubiprostone — 24 μg PO BID. Has shown reno-protective signals.

5-HT4 Agonist

  • Prucalopride — improves bowel function when conventional laxatives are inadequate.
    Dose reduction required in CKD: Reduce to 1 mg once daily (instead of standard 2 mg).

Dual-Benefit Agent (Hyperphosphatemia + Constipation)

Tenapanor — NHE3 inhibitor; reduces intestinal phosphate and sodium absorption, offering added benefit in CKD patients with concurrent hyperphosphatemia and constipation.
Parameter Details
US FDA Dosing 30 mg PO twice daily, taken just prior to morning and evening meals. Adjust based on serum phosphorus and GI tolerability. Do not take right before hemodialysis.
Japanese Dosing Start 5 mg BID; titrate to 10, 20, or 30 mg BID at ≥1 week intervals based on response.
Key Evidence Significantly increases weekly stool frequency (4.7 → 8.2 in those with ≤6 stools/week) and improves BSFS scores (2.7 → 4.0). Laxative use decreased from 58.2% to 35.6% at week 23 in one RCT.
Adverse Effect Diarrhea is primary adverse effect; may require discontinuation. Gradual dose titration may help mitigate.
Additional Benefit Reduces phosphate binder pill burden; lanthanum carbonate prescriptions decreased significantly in tenapanor groups.

Rectal Therapies (PRN Only)

  • Glycerin or bisacodyl suppository — 1 suppository PR PRN (onset 15–60 min).
  • Microlax enema — onset 2–15 min. Do NOT use Fleet enema (phosphate-containing).
  • Not recommended for chronic use.
If no BM for ≥7 days: Rule out fecal impaction and bowel obstruction before escalating therapy. Consider manual disimpaction if indicated.

👥 Step 4 — Special Population Nuances

🔄 Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

▶
Critical: Constipation in PD can cause catheter migration and drainage failure. Laxatives are often used chronically and proactively.
  • Senna glycosides and bisacodyl may be necessary as initial therapy in PD patients.
  • Synbiotic supplementation (fructo-oligosaccharides + Lactobacillus strains + Saccharomyces boulardii) has been shown to relieve chronic constipation and reduce laxative use in PD patients (36.8% suspended laxatives, 42.1% reduced dose).
  • Fiber supplementation (e.g., fructo-oligosaccharides) should be considered in PD patients with low fiber intake or constipation not resolving with dietary measures alone.
  • Tenapanor may be a useful agent in PD patients for both phosphate reduction and constipation management.

🫀 Kidney Transplant Recipients

▶
Diarrhea (commonly from mycophenolate) is more problematic than constipation in transplant recipients.
  • If constipation occurs: docusate 200 mg PO BID. If no BM after 48 h, add sennosides 24 mg PO BID or PEG 3350 17 g PO daily.
  • If no BM after 72 h, continue sennosides or PEG 3350 and add bisacodyl suppository 10 mg PR or glycerin suppository PR.
  • Review medications — tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and corticosteroids can all affect GI motility.

🕊️ Advanced / Palliative Renal Disease

▶
  • Fentanyl causes less constipation than other opioids — preferred if opioid therapy is necessary.
  • Focus on comfort and quality of life; aggressive bowel regimens may be appropriate.
  • Consider methylnaltrexone (peripheral opioid antagonist) if opioid-induced constipation is refractory, though renal dosing data are limited.

⚠️ Hyperkalemia Concern

▶
  • Constipation can worsen hyperkalemia by increasing intestinal potassium absorption time.
  • Regular bowel movements (every 1–2 days) help minimize hyperkalemia risk.
  • If potassium binders (e.g., patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) are used, they may themselves cause constipation — monitor and adjust.
